Chapter 1

## United States Army Reserve Mission, Market, and Value


## Proposition


## INTRODUCTION

1-1. The United States Army Reserve (USAR)’s mission is to provide combat-ready units and Soldiers to the
Army and the joint force across the full spectrum of conflict. USAR recruiting is therefore a critical piece of
building the Army’s end strength. United States Army Recruiting Command (USAREC) and its
subcommands carry the mission to find and recruit qualified men and women to join the USAR.

## BACKGROUND

1-2. The USAR was formed in April 1908 to provide a reserve of medical officers to the Army. In 2025,
nearly 170,000 USAR Soldiers and 11,000 Civilians are present across 50 states, five United States territories,
and deployed to 23 countries around the world. The USAR contains nearly half of the Army’s maneuver
support and a quarter of its force mobilization capacity at only 6% of the total Army budget.
1-3. The USAR is comprised of three components: the Selected Reserve (SELRES), the Individual Ready
Reserve (IRR), and the retired reserve.
o
The SELRES is divided into troop program units (TPUs), Active Guard and Reserve (AGR)
Soldiers, and Soldiers in the Individual Mobilization Augmentee (IMA) program. SELRES Soldiers
perform part-time duties, most commonly one weekend a month and two weeks of active-duty
training per year, and it offers its own set of tangible and intangible benefits. They are called to
active duty on a rotational basis to meet the full-time needs of the Army and the nation. This is the
portion of the USAR that USAREC recruits to fill.
o
The IRR consists of trained Soldiers who previously served in the Regular Army or SELRES. They
may be called upon, if needed, to replace Soldiers in active duty and USAR units. It is comprised
primarily of Soldiers who need to fulfill their military service obligation (MSO). Soldiers who have
completed their MSO can elect to remain in the IRR, if eligible, or change their status to retired
reserve.
o
The retired reserve is made up of all Reserve Soldiers who earn a military retirement. Members who
accumulate 20 or more years of qualifying service are eligible for reserve retirement (also called
non-regular retirement) when they reach age 60 or, in some cases, a lesser qualifying age. These
members are not discharged and may be called back to active service under certain circumstances,
such as war or national emergency. See https://www.usar.army.mil/Retirement/ for more
information.

## OPERATIONAL ENVIRONMENT

1-4. Accomplishing the USAR recruiting mission is far more than conducting Army Interviews with potential
applicants. Some of the workflow basics of every recruiter is to:
o
Investigate and analyze the available market and the population in a geographic region that
physically and mentally qualifies for service.
o
Conduct lead-generation activities to obtain the names, addresses, and telephone numbers or email
address of individuals who may have an interest in the USAR.
o
Identify prospects: people who indicate interest in an enlistment or commission to a recruiter directly
or indirectly.
o
Conduct the Army Interview, a formal meeting between a recruiter and a prospect for the purpose
of telling the Army story and counseling them on the benefits of an Army enlistment or commission.
o
Process applicants, including the preparation of forms, mental testing (enlistment processing only),
a medical exam, background investigations, and administering the oath of enlistment.

1-5. Recruiting operations and success in achieving the USAR mission require cultivating markets in their
area of operations (AO) through understanding the operational environment (OE) and how the USAR value
proposition meets market needs.
1-6. Understanding the OE is one of several keys to success in recruiting. Likewise, success in achieving the
USAR mission requires understanding the nature of the mission, such as prior service (PS) and non-prior
service (NPS) requirements, customers (the type of USAR units in the AO), and communities (recruiting
markets) in the AO, and more importantly, the relationships and networks—formal and informal
interconnected groups or associations of people—established within them.
1-7. Unlike the active-duty mission, the USAR mission has unique aspects that recruiters must be aware of
to achieve mission success. While the active-duty mission is not subject to geographical constraints or unit
vacancy requirements, the USAR must consider details like an applicant’s location in proximity to USAR
unit locations, TPU requirements (manpower vacancies), where applicants live, and deployment cycles,
which all can affect recruiting efforts. Recruiting leadership must plan for and consider these aspects when
conducting the USAR mission.
1-8. BEST PRACTICE: “Location, location, location. It’s huge, especially for the Reserve. I can’t stress
enough how important it is to focus on and tailor your tactics to the location of your market. Do your
homework.”
1-9. Geographic focus will reveal pertinent details like military propensity, how receptive the market will be
to recruiting, which incentives are most desired, and what crucial life details are most important to the
individuals living in the AO. For example, people living in an urban OE may generally be more attracted to
financial benefits because of cost of living, whereas people living in a rural OE may generally be more
attracted to the prospect of part-time service because they are family focused. But remember that similar
locations can trump even those common situations—that’s why the in-depth study is so important. USAR
career counselors shared these contrasting experiences about the West Coast:
o
“Washington state is considered a difficult recruiting market because of the presence of large
companies with job opportunities, higher pay, and greater resources. There’s a ‘no need for the
military’ assumption. So other benefits, like travel and adventure, might get a foot in the door.”
o
“California hosts a large Asian population, and education is highly prized in that culture, so they
want to hear about the education benefits the Army can offer.”

## DEFINING THE MARKET

1-10. Understanding who the prospects are and what they are looking for when considering the USAR guides
recruiters to a core audience ready to hear and relate to the Army story. It also arms recruiters with critical
data for building rapport and influence. In every AO, the USAR has access to a broad prospect pool that
includes NPS, PS, and officer prospects.
o
NPS prospects tend to be motivated by education and practical skill development, adventure and
variety, and economic security and stability. They are young people in transition and early career
changers who are more interested in jobs related to health care, business and finance, and
information technology because these choices will give them the means to reach their full potential
while securing their future.
o
PS prospects are generally looking for career advancement, a sense of belonging with the military,
stability and predictability for their family, and education and retirement benefits. They consider the
USAR a way to help them transition to or further their civilian career through the skills they will
learn and value the economic security it offers.
o
Prospective officers (also NPS and PS) are often seeking leadership opportunities, education
benefits, and enhanced responsibilities that will build career prospects in the civilian sector. They
are most often currently college-bound, in college, or a college graduate who is a working
professional, or they may be an enlisted Soldier committed to service who wants to maintain ties to
the Army.
1-11. Recruiters play a vital role in all prospects’ journey to the USAR, acting as an important information
source regardless of the prospect’s point of origin. Refer to UTC 5–03.2 for more information about the role
of a recruiter as a leader, counselor, and mentor.

## VALUE PROPOSITION

1-12. USAR Warrior Citizens can answer the call to serve from wherever they choose while earning medical
and educational benefits, building impactful resumes, and saving for retirement. With locations in 50 states
and five territories, the USAR provides the flexibility to easily move for family, college, or a civilian job
while retaining benefits and training in uniform.
1-13. USAR Soldiers balance future plans, education, and personal goals with a rewarding and challenging
experience that delivers the training, confidence, and skills that employers seek. Across business and
industry, Warrior Citizens hone technical and leadership skills while bringing cutting-edge capabilities to the
Army and the nation.
1-14. The USAR offers a one-of-a-kind experience with life-changing benefits:
o
Augment a civilian career with advanced leadership training
o
Attend college full-time
o
Choose where to live and train close to home
o
Access affordable and low-cost medical, dental, and life insurance benefits
o
Receive educational benefits and tuition assistance
o
Build for the future with a competitive 5% match retirement program
o
Learn advanced technical skills that are marketable in the civilian sector
1-15. The core of the USAR’s value proposition is its ability to provide Soldiers civilian-ready skills, training,
and benefits while serving part time, allowing time and focus for family. This opportunity should form the
cornerstone of USAR marketing and recruiting efforts both to explain the USAR and to differentiate it from
other branches and components of military.

1-16. Figuring out how to exploit the market to empower USAR recruiting is more than determining what
individual prospects want; it’s also about the companies that employ these USAR Soldiers. The USAR offers
unique specialties specifically tailored to a Soldier with a foot in both the Army and civilian workforces,
which opens the door to pitching the USAR to make a prospect more marketable in the civilian sector.
1-17. BEST PRACTICE: “I get good results from explaining to prospects how Soldiers in the Reserve can
access opportunities that make them competitive in the civilian workforce, like guaranteed job training,

o

## TARGETING THE MARKET

1-21. To be effective, marketing and advertising must be tailored to three targets: NPS, PS, and officers—in
addition to aligning the value proposition with common and unique relevant benefits of the USAR to each
target. For all NPS enlisted prospects, civilian-ready skills and training are the primary benefits. Secondary
benefits are important but vary by population segment.
1-22. The first segment, young people in transition, generally includes 17- to 21-year-old students (the
traditional focus of Army recruiting efforts) who are looking for money for college and adventure. This
segment may be particularly attracted to benefits like the Student Loan Repayment Program (SLRP).
1-23. BEST PRACTICE: “Here’s a bonus: If they are headed to school, they can get college credit for basic
training and AIT (Advanced Individual Training). So, although they’re missing a semester of college, they
often come back with more credits than they would have gotten being at school, and they didn’t have to pay
for them, either. Win-win.”
1-24. The second segment, early career changers, generally includes 25- to 29-year-olds looking to advance
their current career or learn new skills to aid their plans. This group will likely be most attracted to the variety
of opportunities the USAR provides as well as the prestige and advantage that being a Soldier will give them
in their career. This segment may be particularly attracted to benefits like cash bonuses.
1-25. The third segment, economic security seekers, tends to include mostly lower to middle income class
citizens and includes many African American and Hispanic women. This group is particularly motivated by
economic security and the wide selection of military occupational specialties (MOS) available to women
versus other Army components. This segment may be particularly attracted to benefits like housing and
healthcare.

1-30. While the PS enlisted market comprises three distinct segments—current active, IRR, and
retired/honorably discharged Soldiers (and occasionally members of sister services)—they share similar
reasons for joining the USAR. These people are “Army believers” who feel a strong connection and loyalty
to the organization where they spent such an important part of their life. While the enhancement of civilian
ready skills to aid their careers is critical, there are other key benefits to highlight, including the connection
they still feel for the Army or the military in general and the opportunity to continue to earn valuable benefits
for retirement and health care.
1-31. BEST PRACTICE: “Bringing people into the IRR can work because they already have the skills and
the clearance, plus I’ve found this segment has higher than normal unemployment because they are so
‘military’ that they have trouble adapting to civilian life. Once they’re in the Reserve, they can qualify for a
miltech (military technician) job.”
1-32. BEST PRACTICE: “The prior-service market is a goldmine for Reserve recruiting. In addition to
having training and experience, they’re usually looking for specific benefits or jobs only the Reserve can
offer. One of these applicants gives me a quick and easy contract. Boom!”
1-33. By employing the eight recruiting functions, recruiters can develop operational plans to target the PS
market:
o
Mission Command. Command and Control. Know the command directives and policies regarding
the PS mission.
o
Intelligence and Market Analysis. Identify and locate the market. Examples: veterans
organizations, United States Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) offices.
o
Lead Generation and Prospecting. To reach out to the PS market, determine the best:

How: telephone, face-to-face, email, social media

When: mornings, evenings, weekends

Where to post materials/advertising the area: VA office, American Legion, post retention office,
college veterans clubs
o
Interviewing. Determine if there is time to conduct an on-the-spot interview and what message to
deliver.
o
Processing. Confirm what documents are needed to process a PS applicant.
o
Leading Future Soldiers. Ask if they know any veterans in their area and if any of their influencers
are PS.
o
Training and Leader Development. Request assistance on how to prospect for and process PS
applicants. Identify scheduled training events.
o
Sustainment. Account for assets such as Total Army Involvement in Recruiting contacts, medical
recruiting battalion personnel, and Active Duty for Operational Support (ADOS) Soldiers to use in
conjunction with prospecting efforts.

## BREAKING BARRIERS

1-43. Barriers to accessing vary for enlisted Soldiers and officers. For enlisted, the primary barrier is the
possibility of mobilization and deployment. The USAR does a good job addressing this barrier using
stabilization programs, but recruiters do need to communicate some of the benefits of deployment (e.g.,
housing, education, leadership opportunities, pay increases).
1-44. Another barrier is the perceived lack of employer support. Address this barrier by introducing the
Private Public Partnership Office (P3O). Private Public Partnership (P3) is a jobs-now program, focused on
newly accessed USAR and transitioning active-duty Soldiers, that collaborates with private and public
industry and agencies. P3O, in the Office of the Chief of Army Reserve (OCAR), manages the program and
assists USAR Soldiers with finding civilian employment opportunities by offering resume development and
interview tips. P3O works to properly align military skills with civilian sector jobs. They access exclusive
hiring events and connect the Soldier to career opportunities offered by P3 partner organizations. Refer to
https://www.usar.army.mil/P3/ for more information.
1-45. While officers share the barriers of deployment, employer support, and benefit levels, they also cite the
unique barrier of confusion over the terms of their commitment. Therefore, it will be important that current
officers and recruiters clearly understand the terms of commitment to eliminate any confusion about when
the commitment is complete.

## SUMMARY

1-46. Achieving success in the USAR mission requires a keen understanding of nuances within the AO, as
well as thorough grounding in the prospect market, how to access it, and how to find the appropriate audience
with a tailored value proposition. Without understanding the OE and defining and targeting the appropriate
markets, the USAR recruiting mission is at risk.
