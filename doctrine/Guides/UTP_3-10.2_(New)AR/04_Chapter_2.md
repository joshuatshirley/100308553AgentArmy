## Networks


## INTRODUCTION

2-1. To identify and set up networks, recruiters must grow and maintain connections throughout the
community, supported by Army resources. Community-based grassroots networks are particularly effective
in overcoming barriers based on fear, lack of knowledge, and misinformation. A well-designed program can
engage, equip, sustain, and utilize credible advocates—people who promote the interests of the Army—to
support the annual operation plan.

## CONNECTING TO NETWORKS

2-2. This requires regular contact with and, even better, consistent involvement in various networks. The
recruiter is in effect creating and cultivating force multipliers. Meeting and maintaining relationships with
Army accessions partners, TPUs, and a wide range of community partners (CP) is especially important.

## TROOP PROGRAM UNITS

2-8. The nucleus of the USAR recruiting mission is the TPU, because it is the vital link to USAR Soldiers,
personnel, and resources. A strong relationship with the TPU commander, Soldiers, and full-time staff is
critical to the success of the USAR recruiting mission.
2-9. Each TPU has a wealth of experience and knowledge in its Soldiers. While these individuals are Soldiers
during battle assemblies, the rest of the month they are educators, police officers, employers, community
leaders, etc. These Soldiers must be identified and utilized as force multipliers to not only achieve the USAR
recruiting mission but ultimately to build the strength of the USAR.
2-10. Utilizing TPU members in the daily business of recruiting is smart business. Having a respected
teacher, friend, or relative present when giving a presentation or conducting an Army Interview will validate
and give credibility to the Army story. TPU Soldiers live and work in all sectors of the civilian community,
and recruiters need to work with them to cultivate the market. Utilizing the right people will make all the
difference in TPU partnerships and mission success (see figure 2–2).

COMMAND AND STAFF
2-11. The TPU commander is the most important person to get to know. The commander is the approval
authority for all actions involving the unit’s Soldiers and resources. A recruiter, their station commander, and
company leadership should meet with the unit commander as often as possible to maintain a constant, strong
working relationship. A strong relationship will foster better cooperative efforts for applicant processing and
participation in R2PC meetings. Refer to paragraph 2–15 of this publication and AR 601–2 for more
information.
2-12. The TPU’s full-time staff, such as the Army Reserve Administrator (ARA) or AGR noncommissioned
officer (NCO), is also a vital link to partnership as well as an essential part of the applicant processing cycle.
TPUs have full-time staff who manage the operation of the facility, the unit, and its Soldiers, providing
critical continuity. Each staff member has a specific job to keep the unit operational. The recruiter should
know these people; these people should know the recruiter. They should also know to refer all questions
regarding USAR enlistment to that recruiter.
2-13. The ARA is the unit’s records manager. All personnel actions go through the ARA for approval or
disapproval by the commander. The ARA will help obtain copies of documents needed, manage unit
vacancies, and assist with vacancy loads, as well as acceptance letters or renegotiation requests.
2-14. BEST PRACTICE: “Place more emphasis on getting to know the ARA. It’s important because they
have signature authority in the absence of the commander. That means you don’t have to wait for the
commander for signed paperwork after they’ve given the ARA verbal approval for something like a release,
transfer, or authorizations and logistics.”
2-15. The Facility Manager and Supply NCO are good to know when utilizing the Army Reserve Center and
equipment like uniforms for Future Soldiers or events such as Future Soldier functions or training. All
requests for building utilization go through the Facility Manager.
2-16. The Retention NCO is another important full-time staff member. Retention NCOs are critical because
they have close contact with each Soldier in the unit and can assist in providing referrals, writing PS contracts,
monitoring Future Soldiers waiting for their ship date, and accessing various systems, such as the Recruit
Quota System (REQUEST) (see chapter 4) and Integrated Personnel and Pay System Army (IPPS-A) (see
chapter 4).
2-17. BEST PRACTICE: “The Retention NCO can assist with issues such as DEERS (Defense Enrollment
Eligibility Reporting System) and fail-to-gain to the TPU.”

## COMMUNITY PARTNERS

2-35. When properly informed and motivated, community leaders can become passionate advocates and
trusted sources of information. Over time, they can positively influence local perceptions of the Army and
build an environment that is more favorable to the Army’s recruiting message. Because of their status within
the community, advocates also can be very effective in helping overcome recruiting obstacles such as lack
of access to schools. Alignment between what the Army says and what respected and trusted community
leaders are saying creates credibility and impact.
2-36. Recruiters are the face of the Army to many within the communities and are often the highest-ranking
military officer or NCO some people will ever meet in person. Establishing and developing networks with
CPs provides wide-reaching and influential contacts across the AO. CPs could be members of the supported
TPU and can provide contacts and leads within the USAR market.
2-37. CPs are good sources for obtaining introductions for hard-to-penetrate schools, school districts,
professional groups, local communities, and organizations. Find any and every reason to recognize these
individuals and officials. Accessing schools, communities, and organizations can be difficult at times; a CP
can make all the difference. Remember, they are respected leaders or influencers in the community.
2-38. BEST PRACTICE: “The more people I can connect with and get to help extends my reach. I know
what force multipliers are, and I have no problem using them to save time and get leads.”
2-39. Partnerships, as outlined in UTC 5–03.3, require establishing relationships and applying interpersonal
communication with various organizations and individuals within the recruiting AO. These relationships are
enduring and long-term, not temporary, based on a recruiter’s immediate needs. They require constant care
and maintenance.
ARMY RESERVE AMBASSADORS
2-40. These official USAR representatives are a key networking component whose current priority is to
support recruiting initiatives. Ambassadors are appointed by the Chief of Army Reserve (CAR) to a three-
year term to serve as special government employees who voluntarily represent the CAR without salary, wage,
or benefits. For authority purposes, all ambassadors are considered equivalent to a major general. The primary
role of an ambassador is to develop awareness and advocacy for the USAR by building bridges of
communication to communities across the Nation.
2-41. Army Reserve Ambassadors are helping to grow the future force by attracting new talent: recruiting
and retaining junior leaders through educational benefits such as the Guaranteed Reserve Forces Duty
Minuteman Scholarship program, which covers up to four years of full tuition and fees (undergraduate or
graduate programs) or provides thousands of dollars for room and board at colleges and universities served
by an Army ROTC program.
2-42. Ambassadors have the advantage of strategic timing, long-term relationships, and continued dialogue
with key community stakeholders and centers of influence (COI) within their respective AOs. COIs are
individuals or groups that can influence or impact recruiting operations. These are primarily educators

## ACCESSIONS PARTNERS

UNITED STATES ARMY RECRUITING COMMAND
2-44. USAREC is responsible for the Army accessions enterprise with the mission of manning both the
active-duty Army and the USAR. Headquarters (HQ) USAREC, located at Fort Knox, Kentucky, provides

responsible for the ROTC; and the Army Enterprise Marketing Office (AEMO), which oversees the MEB.

thousand recruiters spread across the nation, USARD carries the bulk of the recruiting mission. USARD is
also headquartered at Fort Knox. Refer to https://armyeitaas.sharepoint-mil.us/sites/TR-USAREC-HQ for
more information.

## RECRUITING AND RESERVE PARTNERSHIP COUNCIL

2-51. The R2PC is a perpetual partnership forum designed to improve communication and synchronize
recruiting efforts among USAREC elements, supported USAR units, USACC detachments representing
ROTC programs in surrounding universities, and ARCG personnel within their respective AOs. HQ
USAREC and the deputy chief of staff, Army G–1, guide the R2PC with the goal of building and sustaining
unit personnel strength and readiness. Refer to AR 601–2 for more information.
2-52. An R2PC should convene annually at the recruiting brigade level and semiannually at the recruiting
battalion level. Recruiting battalions covering large geographical areas should consider hosting additional
R2PCs to meet the needs of supported USAR units. Multiple sessions enable smaller group discussions and
personal training. Recruiting companies should not only attend the battalion events but also host R2PCs
beyond the battalion semiannual requirement to bridge the gap between battalion R2PCs. Medical recruiting
battalion commanders and command sergeants major will host at least one R2PC annually.

## SUMMARY

2-57. Understanding and developing a USAR recruiting network is critical to achieving the USAR mission.
These networks center on relationships with the supported TPU unit and their leaders and community
partnerships. Recruiting for the USAR will remain difficult without continuously coordinating with TPUs
and their leaders, building strong networks with good relationships within the community, and interfacing
with CPs. Integrating into the community is not only essential to success, but also beneficial to the community
and TPUs through shared effort.
